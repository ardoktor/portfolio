2024 — 2025 · Stopped

MentAI

MentAI was a personalized mentorship app: daily guidance, reflection prompts, and adaptive tasks meant to help people make steady progress on cognitive and productivity goals. A small team of close friends built it — my first end-to-end product, and the project that pulled me into building products at all. We stopped it after MVP testing: the users we spoke to didn't have the need we had designed for. This page is the postmortem.

What we actually built

The idea worth keeping was structured user state instead of chat history. A user wasn't a transcript; they were a profile, goals, daily inputs, assigned tasks, progress signals, and a weekly summary that acted as compressed memory — each interaction read that state and wrote back to it. Serverless functions on Google Cloud handled the asynchronous work: processing a day's input, scheduled nudges, weekly reviews. The LLM sat inside controlled pipelines as a reasoning component — summarizing input, extracting signals, shaping the next day's guidance — rather than as an open chat. The architecture worked. That is not the same as the product working.

What went wrong

Over-structured UX

The early product asked users to fill forms about their day — mood, energy, goals — because structured input made the state model clean. Users felt boxed in, and said so. We flipped it: free-text input, with structured extraction after the fact. The complaint went away and onboarding got smoother — but the people who had bounced didn't come back. We had fixed the friction, not the reason to return.

Over-generation

Left to generate freely, the guidance came out inconsistent — different tone every day, motivational filler, verbosity that read as noise. We moved to templates, structured outputs, and validation, and the outputs became consistent and genuinely better. It changed nothing measurable. Nobody had been leaving because of tone; polishing the answer polished a thing people weren't asking for.

Unmeasurable progress

"Is this helping?" had no honest answer for months — mentorship progress is fuzzy, and we let it stay fuzzy. Eventually we defined a few hard signals: streaks, task completion, friction points. That fix did work, in the least comfortable way possible — the dashboard stopped being arguable, and what it showed was thin engagement that no feature shipped afterwards moved.

Said versus did

Interviews were encouraging to the end. People liked the idea, said they wanted a system like this, gave thoughtful feedback. Usage disagreed: they tried it, drifted, and didn't miss it. We spent too long weighing what testers said over what they did — the single most expensive lesson on this page.

Why we stopped

Once the signals were honest, the decision mostly made itself. The gap wasn't a missing feature or a rough interface — the people we could reach didn't have the daily need the whole design assumed, and no iteration on our side could manufacture it. What it changed

The next two projects were built against this one's mistakes. ConsulDent started from a domain expert with the need in her own workday — my co-founder brings the cases — instead of from an idea looking for users; demand gets validated before architecture now, not after. The audience flipped from broad consumer to narrow professional. And the community work around MentAI — presenting it publicly, running the Instagram, the Karşıyaka founder collective — taught us that distribution is a separate problem from building, which is why ConsulDent went to cohorts of real dentists before anything else.
