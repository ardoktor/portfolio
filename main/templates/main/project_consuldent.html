ConsulDent

ConsulDent is a clinical support system for dentists, built together with a practising dentist — my co-founder — rather than for an imagined one. She brings the cases, the vocabulary, and the veto; I build the system. It answers clinical questions from retrievable sources and returns structured output that slots into the day's work: visit preparation, clinical notes, patient communication. It is live on the App Store and in paid use.

The problem

A dentist's knowledge isn't missing — it's scattered. Guidelines live in one PDF, dosages in a textbook, the department's preferred protocol in someone's notes, and none of it is reachable in the two minutes between patients. The failure mode isn't ignorance; it's friction: looking something up costs more than it should, so it gets skipped, half-remembered, or asked in a WhatsApp group.

Working with my co-founder made the shape of the problem concrete. Watching her actual day made one thing obvious that I would have gotten wrong alone: the bottleneck isn't answering a question, it's what happens after — the answer has to become a note, a preparation sheet, or something a patient can understand. Why it isn't a chat box

The obvious build was a chat interface over a RAG pipeline. We didn't ship that, for a product reason rather than a technical one: chat pushes the hard work back onto the dentist. She has to phrase the question well, judge whether the fluent answer is actually grounded, and then manually turn the transcript into something usable. Mid-procedure, none of that is realistic.

So the unit of output is a document, not a message. Each task — visit preparation, a treatment explanation, a clinical note, patient-facing wording — has a defined output shape with the source references attached. Structure makes answers checkable (the citation is right there), reusable (a note is a note, not a chat excerpt), and honest about scope: the system does defined jobs instead of pretending to converse about anything.

Voice input exists for the same reason. Typing mid-procedure isn't possible with gloves on; speech is the only interface the clinic actually allows. Whisper turns it into text, and the same structured pipeline takes over.

Architecture

Two paths share one store. Offline, sources are ingested, chunked along their own structure, embedded, and written to Postgres. At query time, retrieval pulls candidate sections, a reasoning step composes and validates the answer against them, and the output layer renders the task's structure.

Decisions and trade-offs

Vector storage

Supabase with pgvector, not a dedicated vector database.

The alternatives were the usual ones — Pinecone, Qdrant, Weaviate. I chose pgvector because it keeps vectors and application data in the same Postgres instance: one connection, one auth story, and retrieval results join directly against chunk metadata in SQL instead of being stitched together across two systems. At this corpus size that simplicity wins outright. The cost is a known ceiling: pgvector's index performance won't match a purpose-built HNSW store at tens of millions of vectors, and heavy retrieval competes with ordinary app queries for the same database. If the corpus grows past what one instance handles comfortably, this decision gets revisited — knowingly.

Tenancy

A single-tenant system with a dentist_id seam, not multi-tenant infrastructure.

The honest version of where the product is: one paying user class, cohorts of testers, no clinics with isolation requirements yet. Building multi-tenant infrastructure now would have been engineering for a customer that doesn't exist. Instead the system runs as a singleton with a dentist_id threaded through every table from day one — a seam, not a feature. It buys shipping speed today and a defined migration path later. The cost is real: some queries quietly assume one user's corpus, and turning the seam into actual tenancy will be a project, not a config change. I consider that debt taken on deliberately, at a known interest rate.

Chunking

Section-aware chunking, after naive splitting failed.

The first pipeline did what every tutorial does: fixed-size chunks with overlap. For clinical texts that broke in a specific, dangerous way — a dosage table separated from the heading that says which patient group it applies to, contraindications split from the indication they belong to. Retrieval then returned sections that were topically right and clinically wrong. The fix was chunking along the documents' own structure: chunks keyed to headings, carrying their section path as metadata the retrieval and validation steps can check. The cost is ingestion effort — every new source format needs mapping work before it enters the corpus, so the library grows slower than a naive pipeline would allow. In this domain that trade is not close.

Output contract

Schema-validated structured output, not free-form generation.

Every task type has a defined output schema; generation is validated against it and retried on a miss. That's what makes outputs land as usable artifacts — a note, a prep sheet — with source references attached, and it caps how far a hallucination can travel: a claim without a retrievable source has nowhere to sit in the structure. The cost is rigidity. The system does its defined jobs well and open-ended questions worse, and every new output type is engineering work — schema, template, validation — rather than a prompt tweak. For a clinical tool I'll take predictable-and-narrow over flexible-and-occasionally-wrong.

Payments

Lemon Squeezy as merchant of record, not Stripe.

Stripe doesn't serve Turkey-origin businesses without a foreign entity, and incorporating abroad to run a test cohort's payments would have been absurd. Lemon Squeezy acts as merchant of record — it owns tax handling and card acceptance globally, which is how "payments live" became true in days instead of months. The costs: a noticeably higher fee than raw card processing, less control over the checkout, and payout delay. It's the right tool for this stage and probably the wrong one at scale; that switch is a future problem I accepted on purpose.

What was hard

Plausible-but-wrong retrieval was the defining fight. Embeddings are good at topical similarity and indifferent to clinical correctness — the paragraph about the adult protocol ranks beautifully for a question about a child. The chunking rework above was half the answer; the other half is validation that checks composed answers against the retrieved sections and a hard rule at the output layer: no source, no claim. When grounding fails, the honest behavior is to return less — a narrower answer with references — rather than a fluent guess. Getting the system to prefer "less" took longer than building the happy path.

The corpus itself is a constraint nobody warns you about: dental sources are a mix of Turkish and English, and embedding quality is not symmetric across them. Queries arrive in Turkish clinical shorthand; the best source for the answer may be an English textbook section. Cross-language retrieval works, but it needed deliberate evaluation with my co-founder reviewing outputs case-by-case — her review queue, on top of her actual job, was the scarcest resource in the project.

And the unglamorous part: none of this mattered until it survived a real clinic. A tool that needs two free hands loses to habit instantly — which is why voice input stopped being a nice-to-have — and a dentist who has asked a colleague for twenty years doesn't switch because your architecture is sound. Where it stands

Live on the App Store, roughly fifty dentists reached across three test cohorts, payments running. The mobile release moved usage from "I'll check at my desk" toward the chairside moments the product was designed for. The current cohort is about retention: whether the workflow outputs — not the answers — are what people come back for. Deliberately not built yet: clinic-level accounts and multi-dentist operations. That's what the tenancy seam is waiting for, and it stays unbuilt until a clinic asks.
